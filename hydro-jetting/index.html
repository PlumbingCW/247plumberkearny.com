When a stopped line needs jetting right away

Most jetting is planned. But a kitchen line that is packed with grease, or a main that keeps closing up, can leave you without a working building. Here is what helps in the meantime.

  1. Stop using the affected drains. In a restaurant, that means pausing dishwashing and pot sinks until the line moves.
  2. Do not add hot water and dish soap hoping to melt a plug. It often just carries grease further down where it hardens again.
  3. Find and clear access to the cleanout so the crew can set up the hose without moving equipment.
  4. If water is on the floor, switch off nearby equipment and keep people out of the wet area.
  5. Call (973) 488-5410 and tell us the building type, how often the line has clogged and whether anyone has cabled it recently.

Jetting is not always the right tool, and we say so when it is not. A camera look often decides that in the first few minutes.

What hydro jetting actually is

A jetting machine pumps water through a flexible hose at high pressure and high flow. The hose ends in a nozzle with several angled ports. Some ports shoot forward to break a blockage, and others shoot backward to pull the hose along the pipe and to blast the walls on the way out.

The water does the work, not a blade. That makes jetting good at getting rid of coatings, because it scours the entire circumference of the pipe. A cable cuts a hole the width of its head. A jet can restore much of the pipe's original bore if the pipe is sound.

Nozzle types and what each is meant for

The nozzle is chosen to match the blockage and the pipe. Here is how the common ones differ.

Why we put a camera in first

Pressure is only useful in a pipe that can take it. Before we jet a line with a history of trouble, we prefer to look at it. A camera on a push rod shows the pipe material, the scale, the joints and any sag or break.

That look protects you in two ways. It stops us from putting a high-pressure hose into a pipe that is cracked or collapsed, which could make things worse. And it lets us show you the same line after jetting, so you can see the difference instead of taking our word for it.

Pipes that should not be jetted

Jetting is aggressive on purpose. Some pipes cannot handle it. A section that has collapsed will not pass a hose, and forcing it risks a wider failure. Pipe that is cracked or has a pulled-apart joint can leak jetting water into the soil or lift further.

Fiber conduit sold decades ago as Orangeburg pipe, which is bonded paper and tar, softens and deforms and does not tolerate high pressure well. In those cases we choose a gentler method or recommend a repair. A good plumber does not use a tool because it is on the truck. We use the one that fits the pipe.

Jetting is routine maintenance, but a repair that follows it falls under the National Standard Plumbing Code, 2024 edition, which Kearny says plumbing plans must follow. Permitted work receives an inspection.

Grease, scale and roots: what jetting clears best

Grease is the best case for a jet. It builds on the pipe wall layer by layer and is exactly what cables leave behind. Water at pressure shears it off and carries it to the main. For commercial kitchens, grease also moves downstream, so jetting is often paired with a check of the grease trap.

Scale is the rough mineral and rust crust that cast iron develops over decades. Roots are a mixed case. Jetting cleans the strands out of the line, but it cannot seal the joint they came through, so roots can return unless the opening is repaired.

Good candidates for scheduled jetting

Jetting makes the most sense as maintenance, not just as a response to a crisis. These situations call for it.

  • A kitchen line, in a restaurant or a large home, that clogs again within weeks of cabling.
  • A multifamily building where many fixtures share one stack and one lateral.
  • A cast iron line with rough interior scale that catches paper.
  • A main line where the camera shows thick grease, soap or mineral buildup.
  • A property with a pattern of backups that cabling has not solved.

If a line backs up only occasionally and the camera shows a clean, sound pipe, a simple cable is enough, and jetting would cost more than it earns.

After the jet: what a clean line should do

When the job is done, water should flow freely and quietly with no gurgling at other fixtures. We run water through the line to confirm. If we ran a camera, you will see the cleaned pipe, and we will point out anything we noticed, such as a joint, a sag or a spot that holds water.

Jetting does not make a pipe new. A line with a belly will still hold water in the low spot. We tell you that and explain whether the pipe needs repair, such as sewer line repair, or whether routine cleaning will keep it working.
