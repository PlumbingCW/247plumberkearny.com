Burst pipe: what to do first

Every minute that water runs adds to the damage, so the first job is to stop it. A typical supply line can release many gallons in a few minutes. Our crews are dispatched around the clock, and same-day appointments are available for slow leaks that are not actively flooding.

  1. Close the main shutoff valve for the house. It is usually near the water meter or where the pipe enters the basement. Turn a gate valve clockwise or a ball valve a quarter turn.
  2. Open the faucets at the lowest and highest points in the house to drain the lines and relieve pressure.
  3. Turn off the water heater's power or gas if the burst is on a hot line, so the heater does not run dry and overheat.
  4. Switch off breakers for any room with water near outlets or fixtures. Do not stand in water to reach the panel. If the panel is wet, call the electric utility.
  5. Call (973) 488-5410. Tell us where the water is coming from and whether the main valve stopped the flow.

If the main valve will not turn or does not stop the flow, tell us at once. The curb valve at the street belongs to the water utility and should not be touched without them.

Why pipes burst

The usual story is freezing. Water expands about 9 percent when it turns to ice, and the pipe usually does not split where the ice sits. It splits where pressure builds between the ice plug and a closed faucet. Pipes in exterior walls, unheated crawl spaces, garages and basements near windows are the most exposed.

Pipes also fail without cold. Old galvanized steel rusts from the inside until a thin spot gives way. Copper develops pinholes from corrosion. Water hammer, high water pressure and a poor solder joint can all split a fitting. Identifying the cause matters because the same cause will hit the next section of pipe.

Pipe material and how each one bursts

The material in your walls decides what failed, how it looks and how it is repaired.

How a burst pipe is repaired

After the water is off, we drain the line and cut out the damaged section with a tubing cutter or a saw, leaving clean, round ends. A spot repair joins new pipe with a coupling that matches the material. On copper, that is a soldered or pressed fitting. On PEX, it is a crimp ring or expansion sleeve.

We then restore pressure slowly and watch every joint, because a second weak spot often shows up only when pressure returns. Air is purged from the lines, and we leave the access open for a short time to confirm nothing weeps.

Thawing a frozen pipe without making it worse

If a pipe has frozen but not yet split, you may still be able to save it. Open the faucet nearest the freeze so water and steam can escape as the ice melts. Warm the pipe slowly, starting at the faucet end and working toward the frozen spot, using a hair dryer or warm towels.

Never use an open flame, a torch or a propane heater. They scorch framing and can melt soldered joints. If you cannot find the frozen section or the pipe is inside a wall, stop and call. A pipe can split as it thaws, and the water will not show until the ice melts.

After the repair: protect the house and the claim

Once the water is stopped and the pipe is fixed, a few steps keep a bad night from becoming a bad month.

  • Photograph and video every damaged area before you start cleanup, then keep receipts.
  • Call your insurance company soon. Burst pipe damage from freezing is commonly covered as sudden damage, but policies differ.
  • Dry out wet materials fast. Fans and dehumidifiers help, and wet drywall or insulation often needs to come out.
  • Check the other pipes that share the same wall or run for soft spots or leaks.
  • Insulate exposed pipes in cold areas and keep a slow drip of water running on freezing nights in exposed lines.

If you cannot see where the water went, a leak detection visit can confirm the line is tight.
