Faucet will not shut off: what to do now

Running water you cannot stop is the situation this page is for. Work through these steps in order. They apply at night and on weekends as much as on a weekday, and the same after-hours line handles all of them.

  1. Find the shutoff valves under the sink, two small oval or round handles on the lines going up to the faucet. Turn each clockwise until it stops.
  2. If the valves are stuck or leak when turned, go to the main water valve, where the line enters the basement or utility room, and close it fully.
  3. Open the lowest faucet in the house, such as a basement sink or tub, to drain the pressure from the pipes.
  4. Put a bucket under any spray, move items out of the cabinet and switch off power to outlets near wet areas if you can do so safely.
  5. Call (973) 488-5410 and tell us whether it is the faucet itself or the valve below it that has failed.

Do not force an old valve past its stop. A seized stem can snap and turn a drip into a flood.

Four kinds of faucet, four kinds of repair

The repair depends on how the faucet is built. Compression faucets have separate hot and cold handles and use rubber washers that wear out. Cartridge faucets use a plastic or brass cartridge that slides to mix water. Ball faucets, common on older kitchen sinks, use a slotted ball with springs and seats. Ceramic disc faucets use two polished discs and a seal.

Each type fails differently, so we start by identifying yours. Many repairs mean replacing a washer, cartridge or seat rather than the whole faucet, and we keep common parts on the truck.

Why your faucet drips and what the drip costs

A worn washer or cartridge lets water seep past the seal even when the handle is closed. The drip seems small, but it runs all day and every day. It also leaves mineral stains in the sink and, over time, can wear the seat so the repair gets bigger.

A drip that appears only on hot points to the hot side parts. A drip on both sides suggests a worn cartridge or seat that serves both. We test each side so we replace what has actually failed.

Check these before you call

A few quick checks can save time, and some can fix the problem outright.

  • Unscrew the aerator at the spout tip and clean out grit, since a clogged aerator mimics low pressure.
  • Check whether the low flow is on hot only or cold only, which tells us which supply line or valve to inspect.
  • Look under the sink with a flashlight for drips, wet cabinet floors or green crust on fittings.
  • Note whether the faucet is a single handle or two handles and what brand name is stamped on it.
  • Make sure the shutoff valves under the sink actually turn. If they do not, tell us when you call.

If the faucet is fine but the water under the sink keeps coming back, the culprit may be a drain trap or a supply hose, and a broader leak detection check may be the right next step.

Shutoff valves that will not shut off

Under-sink shutoff valves are rarely turned, so they seize with mineral deposits and the packing dries out. When one fails, the plumber has to shut the main to do the work, which means every fixture in the house goes dry for a while.

We replace old multi-turn valves with quarter-turn ball-type stops when we are already there. They cost little in the scheme of a repair and give you a working shutoff next time something goes wrong. Braided stainless supply lines are replaced at the same visit if the old ones are brittle or bulging.

Kitchen, bathroom, tub and outdoor spigots

Kitchen faucets with pull-down sprayers add a hose and weight to the repair. Bathroom faucets often have a pop-up drain linkage that leaks into the cabinet. Tub and shower valves are different again, because the cartridge sits behind the wall, which is a topic for a separate visit.

Outdoor hose bibs deserve a mention. A bib that drips inside the wall after a cold spell may have frozen, and a quick-closing frost-free model should be considered. If a pipe has split, our burst pipe repair service covers that.
