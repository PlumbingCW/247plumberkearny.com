When a water heater problem is an emergency

No hot water is miserable but rarely dangerous. A water heater turns into an emergency when it is leaking steadily, when you smell gas, or when the relief valve is discharging hot water or steam. We take those calls at any hour, and same-day appointments are available for the slower problems when the schedule allows.

  1. If you smell gas, leave the building right away. Do not touch light switches or the phone inside. Call your gas utility or 911 from outside before you call us.
  2. If the tank is leaking, close the cold water shutoff valve on the pipe entering the top of the heater. If you cannot reach it, close the main water valve for the house.
  3. Switch the heater off. For electric units, turn off its breaker. For gas units, turn the control knob to off or pilot.
  4. If the relief valve is blowing hot water or steam, stay clear of the discharge pipe and do not cap it. Shut off the fuel or power and leave the valve open.
  5. Move stored items off the floor around the heater, then call (973) 488-5410 and tell us the heater's age, fuel type and what you see or hear.

Never plug or cap a relief valve. It is the last safety device on a tank that is holding hot, pressurized water.

Start with what the heater is telling you

A water heater usually gives clear clues before it fails completely. Lukewarm water, rumbling, a pilot that keeps going out, rust-colored hot water or a damp patch at the base each point to a different cause.

Fuel type matters first. A gas unit has a burner, a pilot or electronic igniter, a flame sensor and a gas control valve. An electric unit has two heating elements and two thermostats behind small panels on the side of the tank. The troubleshooting path is completely different for each, so tell us which one you own when you call.

Gas water heaters: pilot, thermocouple and gas valve

A standing pilot flame heats a thermocouple, a small probe that tells the gas valve it is safe to keep gas flowing. When the probe weakens or gets coated in soot, it stops sending a signal and the valve closes. The pilot goes out every time you relight it.

Newer gas units use electronic ignition and a flame sensor instead. The sensor can foul with residue and shut the burner down, and cleaning it often restores operation.

We also check combustion air, the flue draft and the venting, because a heater that backdrafts can put carbon monoxide into the home. If the gas control valve is the failed part, we compare the cost of the valve against the age of the tank before suggesting it.

Electric water heaters: elements, thermostats and the reset button

Electric tanks are simpler to diagnose. We turn off the breaker, remove the access panels and test each element and thermostat with a meter. An element that reads open has burned out, and one that reads grounded to the tank has failed internally.

A red reset button on the upper thermostat trips when the water gets dangerously hot. If it trips again after you press it, something is wrong and pressing it repeatedly is not the fix. A stuck thermostat or a shorted element is the usual reason. We replace the part, restore the breaker and confirm the water reaches a safe set temperature.

Relief valve discharge and expansion pressure

The temperature and pressure relief valve opens when water gets too hot or pressure climbs too high. A valve that drips from time to time may be telling you about thermal expansion rather than failing. When a heater heats water in a closed system, the volume increases and the pressure has nowhere to go.

An expansion tank on the cold supply absorbs that extra volume. If your home has a backflow preventer or a check valve at the meter, an expansion tank is the usual fix. Swapping the valve alone often just starts the dripping again a month later.

Signs a repair is not worth it

We would rather tell you not to spend money than sell a repair that will not last. These are the signs we look for.

  • Water pooling at the base that traces back to the tank wall rather than a fitting.
  • A tank old enough that tank water heaters typically run about 8 to 12 years and yours has passed that range.
  • Rust-tinged hot water, with an anode rod that is gone and a lining that is already breaking down.
  • Repeated repairs within a short window, especially control valve or element failures.
  • A thick layer of sediment on the tank floor that flushing does not move.

If you reach that point, a tankless water heater is one option to consider, though it changes gas sizing, venting and the electrical load.

What to do while you wait for a plumber

If the heater is leaking, keep the shutoff closed and place a bucket or wet vacuum under any drip. If it is a power outage rather than a failure, check the breaker before you call.

Avoid draining a hot tank. Water over about 120 degrees F can scald in seconds. If the tank must be drained for a repair, we let it cool or draw hot water through a hose to a floor drain safely.
