Disposal jammed or leaking: what to do first

A disposal is a motor with sharp parts and a water line, so the first steps are about safety. These apply at any hour, and the dispatch line takes calls at night and on weekends, with same-day appointments for jams that are not flooding.

  1. Turn off the wall switch and unplug the unit from under the sink, or switch off its breaker. Never put a hand or a spoon into the chamber.
  2. If water is leaking from the unit or its connections, close the two shutoff valves under the sink and put a bucket under the leak.
  3. Stop running the tap and the dishwasher. If the sink backs up, the dishwasher drain may be joined to the disposal and can fill the sink.
  4. Do not pour chemical drain cleaner into the sink. It can sit in the disposal and burn whoever opens it next.
  5. Call (973) 488-5410 and tell us what you hear: humming, a clunk or silence. That tells us whether it is jammed, burned out or something else.

A burning smell or smoke means unplug it and leave it off. Do not run it again.

Humming but not spinning: how a jam is cleared

A disposal that hums but does not turn has a jammed flywheel. Most units have a hex socket in the center of the underside. With the power off, an Allen key in that socket lets you rock the flywheel back and forth until it moves freely. Pushing a wooden spoon handle against an impeller edge works as well.

Then press the red reset button on the underside. If it clicks and the unit runs, the overload tripped from the jam. If it hums again right away, something is still caught or the motor is failing.

Leaks under the sink: three places to look

Dry the cabinet floor and the underside of the unit, then run water and watch. A leak at the top, where the sink flange meets the disposal, is a seal problem. A leak where the discharge pipe meets the side of the unit is a loose gasket or bolt. A leak from the bottom or around the reset button means the housing has failed.

The first two are repairable and the third is not. Sitting on a leaking disposal is how a cabinet floor rots, so even a slow leak is worth fixing soon.

What not to put down a disposal

Most disposal trouble starts at the sink. These items cause the majority of jams and clogs.

  • Grease, fat and oil, which cool in the pipe and coat the walls until the line narrows.
  • Fibrous scraps like celery, corn husks and artichoke leaves that wrap around the impellers.
  • Coffee grounds in volume and eggshells, which pack into a paste in the trap.
  • Starchy foods like rice, pasta and potato peels, which swell into a gluey mass.
  • Bones, fruit pits and anything hard that is not food, along with glass, metal and plastic.

Run cold water for a few seconds before and after using the disposal. Cold water keeps grease firm so it is flushed out as a solid. If it clogs, a drain cleaning call clears the line beyond it.

When the disposal is fine and the drain is not

Many calls that sound like disposal failure are actually drain clogs. If the sink holds water and the disposal runs when you test it dry, the blockage is probably in the trap or the line past the disposal. Cabling the line often fixes it without touching the unit.

A dishwasher that drains through the disposal can also push dirty water into the second sink bowl. That means the line is partly blocked, or that the dishwasher hose is not looped high enough under the counter to prevent backflow.

Kearny kitchens, old pipes and grease

Kearny and nearby Lyndhurst have many older homes and two-family buildings with cast iron drain lines that have rough interior surfaces. That is a general pattern for older housing, not a count of any one street. Rough pipe grabs grease and food waste faster, so a disposal that worked fine in a newer house can clog an old line.

Installing a new disposal: what to expect

Replacing a disposal means disconnecting the drain and dishwasher hose, removing the mounting ring, cleaning the old putty off the sink flange, setting the new mount with fresh putty or a gasket, and attaching the unit. Then we reconnect the drain, knock out the dishwasher port if one is needed, and test for leaks.

Adding a disposal where there was none needs a power source and a double-bowl or single-bowl drain that suits it. If you want a new sink and fixtures as well, see plumbing installation and faucet repair.

Safety first: turn off the power at the switch and the breaker before anything goes near the disposal opening, and never put a hand in. According to the manufacturer guidelines for most units, a hex wrench at the bottom frees a jam without touching the blades.
