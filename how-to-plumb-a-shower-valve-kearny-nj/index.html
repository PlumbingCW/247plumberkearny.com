How to Plumb a Shower Valve in Kearny, NJ: Types, Rough-In and Permits

By the Kearny 24/7 Plumbing team. Published October 13, 2026.

A shower valve is the mixing device behind the wall that blends hot and cold supply into one outlet and sets how much flows. Plumbing one means running hot and cold supply to the valve body at the right depth, connecting the outlet to the showerhead and tub spout, and testing everything before the wall is closed.

This guide walks through the choices and the order of work, and it is honest about which parts a careful homeowner can do and which parts belong to a licensed plumber.

Pressure-balance or thermostatic: choosing the valve

Most homes have a pressure-balance valve. A spool or disc inside reacts to a pressure drop on either supply line, such as a toilet flushing, and shifts to keep the ratio of hot to cold steady. It protects against sudden scalding, but the temperature still depends on where you set the handle.

A thermostatic valve senses the actual mixed temperature with a wax or bimetal element and holds it near your setting even when supply pressure or heater temperature changes. It usually has a second control for volume, so one valve can run several outlets such as an overhead head, a hand shower and body sprays.

Valve types side by side

The right choice depends on how many outlets you want, your budget and how much wall depth the framing gives you.

Whatever you pick, buy the valve body and trim from the same maker, because rough valves and trim kits are rarely interchangeable.

Planning the rough-in before you open the wall

Every valve comes with a rough-in sheet that gives the depth from the stud face to the center of the valve body, the height of the outlet ports and the spacing of the supplies. Follow it to the quarter inch, because the trim plate has to sit flush with the finished wall, whether that is tile over backer board or a fiberglass surround.

Mark the valve center at a height you can reach from outside the spray, often around the mid-chest. Then decide where the showerhead arm and the tub spout will land, because the outlet pipes are fixed once the wall is closed. Add a stud-to-stud mounting bracket or a blocked board so the valve cannot move when you turn it.

Tools and materials for the rough-in

Collect everything before you shut the water off, so the house is not without service while you shop.

  • The valve body with its rough-in sheet, plus a matching trim kit and any cartridge it needs.
  • Copper tubing and fittings, or PEX tubing with crimp or expansion fittings, sized to match the valve ports.
  • A tubing cutter, a deburring tool, and a torch with lead-free solder and flux if you are working in copper.
  • Two shutoff valves for the new branch if there is nothing upstream to isolate it.
  • A drop-ear elbow for the showerhead and a stub-out or elbow for the tub spout, firmly strapped to blocking.
  • A test cap or plug set, so you can pressurize the new lines before the wall is closed.

A shower pan or tub drain is a separate trade job. If the drain is being moved, the work widens beyond the valve, and plumbing installation by a licensed plumber is the safer route.

Soldering copper or running PEX

Copper is rigid, tolerates heat well and has a long track record. Soldering it takes practice. Pipe must be cut square, cleaned bright, fluxed and heated evenly until solder is drawn into the joint. Too little heat gives a cold joint that weeps later, and too much heat scorches flux and fails to wet the pipe.

PEX is faster, bends around corners and survives a hard freeze better than copper. Expansion fittings and crimp rings need the correct tool and a gauge to confirm each joint. On many valves, soldering directly to the body can damage internal parts, so check whether the maker wants stub-outs soldered first or provides threaded or sweat adapters.

Order of work from supply to finished wall

The sequence matters because mistakes are cheap to fix before drywall and expensive after. A typical order follows.

  • Shut off the water, drain the branch, and open the wall to expose studs and existing supply lines.
  • Install blocking at the valve and at each outlet location.
  • Set the valve body to the rough-in depth and fasten it to its bracket.
  • Connect hot to the left port and cold to the right port, unless the manufacturer's markings show otherwise.
  • Run the outlet pipe up to the showerhead drop-ear elbow and down to the tub spout stub, and strap each run.
  • Cap the outlets, restore water and pressure test with the valve open, watching every joint for a bead of moisture.
  • Leave the test in place for the time the maker requires before you close the wall.

Leave an access panel when you can

The cartridge inside any valve eventually wears out, and the valve body may need service. A tiled shower with no access means cutting through tile to reach a fault. Where the back of the valve opens onto a closet, hallway or adjacent room, a removable panel behind the wall saves a repair later.

If there is no rear access, keep the trim plate larger than the hole you may need, and note the model number so the right cartridge can be ordered when it is time.

When to call a plumber instead

Replacing a worn cartridge or a trim kit is within reach of many homeowners. Cutting into a wall, soldering inside a closed cavity, relocating a valve or tying into old galvanized supply is where mistakes cause hidden leaks. A weeping joint inside a wall can rot framing for months before a stain shows.
