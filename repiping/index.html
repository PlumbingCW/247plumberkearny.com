Whole House Repiping in Kearny, NJ

Repiping means replacing the water supply pipes inside a house, hot and cold, so you stop chasing leaks and get steady pressure again. It is a planned project in Kearny, NJ, and the first step is an honest look at whether you need it at all.

Signs the supply pipes are wearing out

One leak does not mean a house needs new pipes. A pattern does. These are the signs that point to widespread wear.

  • Rust-colored or brown water when you first open a tap, especially hot water.
  • Low pressure at several fixtures even though the street pressure is fine.
  • Pinhole leaks or green crusty spots on pipes, in more than one place.
  • Repairs that keep reappearing a few feet from the last one.
  • Pipe that looks heavily scaled, flaking or swollen at the fittings.
  • Hot water that takes a long time to arrive or arrives weak.

If you are not sure the problem is the pipes, leak detection can tell you whether one hidden leak is behind the symptoms.

How galvanized, copper and older plastic pipe fail

Galvanized steel is the classic repipe candidate. It is coated in zinc, and over decades the coating wears and rust forms inside. The bore closes up from the inside like a clogged artery, so flow drops long before it leaks. Houses built before about 1960 are the most likely to have it.

Copper lasts a long time but can pit from the inside, especially at elbows where flow is turbulent. Old solder joints can weep. Some polybutylene pipe, a gray plastic used from the late 1970s into the 1990s, is known to fail at fittings and is often replaced as a precaution.

PEX, copper and CPVC for a house repipe

Most whole-house repipes use one of three materials. Here is how they compare in practice.

MaterialStrengthsTrade-offs
PEX (cross-linked polyethylene)Flexible, fewer fittings, quieter, handles some freezing better, quicker to install in finished wallsNot for outdoor sun exposure, and it needs the right fittings and tools
CopperLong track record, tolerates heat, no plastic taste, easy to inspectMore labor, more joints, material cost varies, and can pit in aggressive water
CPVCRigid plastic, resists corrosion, fine for hot and coldBecomes brittle with age, more fittings than PEX, less common for full repipes

We recommend a material based on the house, not a favorite. The code office accepts the approved pipe the plan specifies.

Whichever material, the new supply lines connect to fixtures you may also want updated. See plumbing installation if you plan to add or move any.

Repipe the house or repair a section

A repipe is a big job, so the first question is whether you need one. If the problem is a single failed run and the rest of the pipe is in good shape, a section repair is the honest answer. We will say so.

A full repipe makes sense when galvanized is closing up throughout, when leaks recur in several places, when lead or polybutylene is involved or when you are already opening walls for a renovation. Doing it then spares you from opening them twice.

A partial repipe is also common. Replacing the basement mains and the bathroom risers while leaving a good kitchen run is often the efficient middle ground.

How a repipe goes, day by day

Most single-family repipes take a few days, though size, access and finish work change that. We start by planning the routes and shutting off water at the main. New lines run through basements, crawlspaces and attic or wall cavities, and the old pipe is cut out or abandoned in place if it is not in the way.

We reconnect each fixture, restore water and test the system under pressure. Water is off for part of each work day, and we tell you the schedule so you can plan showers, laundry and cooking. Bring up any medical or household needs when you book.

Walls, patching and living through the work

Opening walls is the part homeowners worry about most. PEX can often be fished through existing cavities with small access holes, which keeps drywall repair modest. Copper and rigid pipe usually need larger openings in some places.

We keep the work area clean and protect floors, and we tell you before work starts which walls will be opened. Patching and painting is a separate trade unless we agree otherwise. Tell us about finished rooms, tile or plaster, because those change how we route the pipe.

Where the service line fits in Kearny

Repiping covers the pipe inside the building. The line from the street to your meter is the service line, and it is a separate project. Kearny's Water Department buys all its water from the North Jersey District Water Supply Commission and posts a lead service line inventory with an address lookup on its page. Lines marked Lead or Galvanized Requiring Replacement are prioritized, and Unknown lines are treated as possibly lead until verified.

Check the lookup before a repipe. If your service line is on the list, tell us, because we would plan the interior work around the connection point. Our page on water service line repair explains that side. Across the river in Belleville, the water report notes that homes built before 1950 may have a lead service line, so Belleville owners should ask the same question.

According to the state Uniform Construction Code, a construction permit is required for plumbing work on existing buildings, with rough piping and final inspections on one- and two-family dwellings. Repiping follows the National Standard Plumbing Code, 2024 edition.

Permits and inspections for a repipe

In New Jersey, plumbing work on an existing building needs a construction permit. The only listed exemption is ordinary maintenance, which includes routine repair. Replacing the supply piping through a house is not routine repair.

The Uniform Construction Code applies plumbing rough piping and final inspections on one and two family dwellings. Kearny's Construction Code Enforcement office, reached at (201) 955-7880 on weekdays, handles applications, and all permits require an inspection. Kearny's plans must follow the National Standard Plumbing Code, 2024 edition. Ask the Kearny construction code office about timing, because processing runs about 7 business days for small jobs and longer for complex ones.

Pressure, flow and what to expect afterward

After a repipe, most people notice stronger flow at the tap, especially where galvanized was choked. Hot water arrives faster when new lines are sized well. Rust-colored water usually clears up because the corroded source is gone.

Pressure also depends on the street supply, so a repipe will not fix a low-pressure supply on its own. If a burst pipe has already damaged a section, we can fold that repair into the same plan.
