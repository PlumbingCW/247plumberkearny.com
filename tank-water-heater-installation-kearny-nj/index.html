Tank Water Heater Installation in Kearny, NJ: A Homeowner Guide

By the Kearny 24/7 Plumbing team. Published October 13, 2026.

A tank water heater installation replaces or adds a storage tank that heats water and holds it ready, using gas or electricity. The work seems simple from the outside, but sizing, venting, safety valves and permits all matter.

Here is what happens during a proper installation, what you can prepare in advance, and where the details tend to go wrong.

What a tank installation includes

The tank is only part of the job. A complete installation drains and removes the old unit, sets the new one on a level base, connects cold and hot water with new flex lines or copper, and fits a temperature and pressure relief valve with a proper discharge pipe.

A gas unit also needs a gas connection with a shutoff and drip leg, plus a flue that draws combustion gases outside. An electric unit needs a correctly sized circuit. Then the tank is filled, purged of air, tested for leaks and set to a safe temperature.

Choosing the right size

Tank size is a balance between how much hot water you use at once and how much space you have. A common range for homes is 30 to 80 gallons. A bigger tank holds more but also loses more heat while it sits.

The first hour rating on the yellow energy label matters more than gallons alone. It tells you how much hot water the heater can deliver in an hour starting full. Count your biggest morning stretch, such as two showers and a dishwasher, and match that to the rating rather than guessing from tank size.

Gas, electric or something else

Your existing fuel is usually the right starting point because changing it adds work. This table shows the trade-offs.

TypeWhat it needsTrade-off
Atmospheric gasGas line, a chimney or vent pipe with proper draft, combustion airLowest equipment cost, but draft problems can cause backdrafting
Power vent gasGas line, electric outlet, plastic vent through wall or roofMore flexible venting, uses power, has a fan that can fail
Electric resistanceDedicated circuit and breaker sized for the unitNo venting at all, usually slower recovery and higher running cost
Heat pump tankElectric circuit, open space and condensate drainEfficient, but needs clearance, cools the room and costs more up front

If your basement or closet is tight, venting and clearance often decide the choice before efficiency does.

What to prepare before the installer arrives

A little preparation saves time and keeps the job moving.

  • Clear a path to the old unit and a work area of about three feet around it.
  • Know where the main water shutoff is and check that it turns. A stuck shutoff valve can stop the job.
  • Move stored items, laundry and anything that could be damaged by water or dust.
  • Find the floor drain, if any, and the nearest place a discharge hose can run.
  • Note the make, model and label data on the old heater, and a photo helps.
  • Expect the hot water to be off for several hours, so plan showers and laundry around it.

If the old unit is already leaking, tell us on the phone so the crew brings the right gear for water cleanup.

Venting and combustion air

Venting is the hidden half of a gas installation. An atmospheric heater relies on hot flue gas rising through a pipe or chimney. If the pipe is too long, too flat or blocked, exhaust can spill back into the house. Combustion air must also reach the burner.

That is why a good installer checks the vent for rust, slope and connections before it is reused. Power vent units push gas out with a fan and use plastic pipe. Either type should be tested with a draft check and a carbon monoxide detector nearby.

Valves, expansion tanks and the details that last

The temperature and pressure relief valve is a safety device. Its discharge pipe must run downward to a safe spot and must never be capped or reduced. A shutoff valve on the cold inlet makes future service easier.

If your home has a backflow preventer or a pressure reducing valve, thermal expansion has nowhere to go and pressure spikes each time the heater runs. An expansion tank absorbs that swing. Dielectric fittings or nipples reduce corrosion between steel and copper. A drain pan helps when the heater sits over finished space.

Permits and inspection for water heaters in Kearny

Kearny's Construction Code Enforcement says that a new or replacement hot water heater needs a zoning permit and a construction permit with the plumbing subcode. All permits require an inspection. Plumbing plans must follow the National Standard Plumbing Code, 2024 edition.

Typical processing is about 7 business days for small jobs and 5 to 7 business days for complex ones, and up to 20 working days is allowed. The permit fee for plumbing is set by ordinance. The front office is at (201) 955-7880, Monday to Friday, 8 am to 4 pm.

State rules point the same way. A construction permit is required for plumbing work on existing buildings, and the only listed exemption is ordinary maintenance. For questions not covered here, ask the Kearny construction code office.

Neighbors across the line may have different steps

Homeowners in North Arlington and Belleville sometimes ask whether the same process applies. Each town runs its own code office, so call theirs before you plan around a date.

Water source can matter too. Hardness and sediment vary by supplier, and harder water tends to build scale on the tank bottom over time. Ask your water utility for its hardness report if you want to know how fast that might happen.

How to check the finished work

When the installer is done, walk through these points together.

  • The tank sits level on a stable base or in a drain pan, with clearance on the sides.
  • The relief valve has a full size discharge pipe that ends near the floor, with no cap or threads.
  • Gas lines have a shutoff and a leak test, and the flue slopes up and is sealed at each joint.
  • Water connections are dry after the first heat cycle. Check them again the next day.
  • The thermostat is set to a safe temperature, often around 120 degrees F for households with children.
  • You know where the gas, electric and water shutoffs are, and you have the manual and warranty paperwork.

Keep the permit and inspection record with the manual. It helps if you ever sell the house or file a claim.
