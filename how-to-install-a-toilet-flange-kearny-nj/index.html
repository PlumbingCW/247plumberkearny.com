How to Install or Replace a Toilet Flange in Kearny, NJ

By the Kearny 24/7 Plumbing team. Published October 13, 2026.

A toilet flange is the ring that bolts your toilet to the floor and connects its outlet to the drain pipe below. When it cracks, rusts through or sits at the wrong height, the toilet rocks, leaks at the base or smells of sewer gas.

This guide explains how to check the flange, which type to buy, and how to replace it step by step. It also marks the points where a repair stops being a weekend job.

What the flange actually does

The flange is a short collar glued or bolted to the end of the drain pipe, with a flat ring on top that lies on the floor. Two closet bolts slide into slots on the ring and hold the toilet down. A wax ring or a wax free gasket sits between the toilet horn and the flange and makes the seal.

Two jobs are bundled together here. The flange anchors the toilet so it cannot shift, and it positions the outlet so the seal stays compressed. If the ring is broken, the bolts have nothing to grip. If the ring sits too low, the seal never closes. Both problems look like a leaking toilet, but the toilet itself is usually fine.

Signs the flange is the problem, not the toilet

Before you buy anything, rule out a loose supply line or a sweating tank. These signs point to the flange or its seal.

  • The toilet rocks or twists when you sit down, even after the bolts are tightened.
  • Water seeps out around the base after a flush, not while the tank refills.
  • A sewer smell comes from the bathroom even though the trap in the sink and tub is full.
  • The floor around the toilet feels soft, or the vinyl or tile is lifting nearby.
  • You see rust flakes or a cracked ring when the toilet is pulled.
  • A previous flange repair never held a seal for long.

A soft floor deserves attention before any flange work. Slow leaks can rot the subfloor, and a new flange needs solid wood to screw into. If the floor flexes, stop and have someone look at it.

Flange types and when each one fits

Hardware stores sell several flanges, and the right one depends on your drain pipe and how damaged the old ring is. This table covers the common choices.

Flange typeBest forWatch out for
PVC or ABS glue-onNew work or a drain pipe cut back and re-glued, 3 or 4 inch pipeNeeds the right cement and a clean cut, and it must match the pipe material
Cast ironOlder homes with cast iron stack, usually lead and oakum or caulked jointsHeavy, can crack, and the old joint takes skill to rework
Stainless steel ringReplacing a cracked top ring while keeping the pipeThin ring on a bad pipe end can still leak

When unsure, take the old flange or a photo of it to the counter. Pipe size and material matter more than brand.

If your flange connects to lead, brass or cast iron, treat it as a plumber's job. Those joints do not behave like modern plastic.

Replacing the flange, step by step

This is the full sequence for a glue-on flange on a plastic drain. Read it through before you start.

  • Shut the water at the stop valve behind the toilet, flush, and hold the handle until the tank is empty. Sponge out what is left.
  • Disconnect the supply line and remove the cover caps and nuts on the closet bolts.
  • Rock the toilet gently to break the seal and lift it straight up. Set it on a towel or cardboard. Expect it to be heavy.
  • Stuff a rag into the open drain to block sewer gas and keep tools from falling in.
  • Scrape off the old wax. Unscrew the old flange from the floor, and cut it away if it is glued to the pipe.
  • Check the pipe end. It must be round, clean and cut square. A split or oval pipe has to be cut back or repaired first.
  • Dry fit the new flange. The top ring should rest on the finished floor, not below it, with the slots lined up front to back.
  • Apply primer and cement to the pipe and the inside of a glue-on flange, push it on with a quarter turn, and hold for the time on the can.
  • Screw the ring to a solid floor with stainless screws, set the closet bolts in the slots, and place the new wax ring.
  • Lower the toilet straight down over the bolts, press firmly, tighten the nuts evenly in small turns, trim the bolts and reconnect the water.

Do not over tighten. Porcelain cracks before it complains. Let the glue cure for the time listed on the can before the first flush, then check the base after a few flushes.

Fixing a flange without cutting the pipe

If the pipe is sound and only the top ring is cracked, a stainless repair ring can bolt over the old flange. Its lower edge sits on the floor, and new screws go through the ring and old flange into the subfloor. It is a quick fix and it avoids cutting the drain.

It does not help when the flange is loose from the pipe, or the pipe has moved. If the pipe wobbles when you push the flange, the connection below the floor has failed. That is the point to stop and check what is under the bathroom, usually from the basement or crawl space.

Getting the height right

Flange height is the detail that most often ruins a good job. The top of the ring should sit on top of the finished floor. If new tile or vinyl was laid over an old flange, the ring may now sit below the floor and the wax cannot close the gap.

A thick wax ring or an extender can bridge a small gap. Stacking several wax rings is a poor fix, because they can slip and smear into the drain. For larger gaps, use a proper extender ring or reset the flange. A flange that is too high is less common but can crack the toilet horn when tightened.

When to hand it to a plumber

Replacing a flange on a plastic drain, on a solid floor, with the toilet over a basement you can see, is a fair DIY job. These situations are better left to a licensed plumber.

The drain is cast iron, lead or galvanized. The floor is soft or has been leaking for a while. The pipe turns out to be loose or cracked below the floor. The toilet is on an upper floor with no access from below. Or you have already reset the toilet twice and it still leaks. Our toilet repair and installation team handles all of these, and a stubborn clog that is not a flange problem falls under drain cleaning.

Permits and ordinary repair

In New Jersey, plumbing work on existing buildings generally needs a construction permit. The state lists only one exemption, ordinary maintenance, which includes routine repair. Swapping a wax ring or resetting a toilet usually falls in that category. Cutting out and replacing drain pipe may not.

Permit rules vary, so ask the Kearny construction code office if you are unsure whether your job qualifies. Neighbors in Harrison and Lyndhurst should check with their own town offices, because each sets its own process.
