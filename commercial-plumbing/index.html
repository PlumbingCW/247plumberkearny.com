Commercial plumbing emergency: what to do first

In a commercial building the first job is limiting the damage and the downtime. These steps work for most after-hours failures.

  1. Find and close the shutoff for the affected area. If you do not know where it is, close the building main.
  2. Stop using the affected restrooms, sinks and floor drains, and block them off so staff and customers stay out.
  3. If water is near electrical equipment, panels or a kitchen hood, shut off the breaker only if you can reach it dry.
  4. Photograph the damage and note the time for your insurer and your landlord before you start cleanup.
  5. Call (973) 488-5410 and tell us the type of business, the floor or unit, and what is backing up or leaking.

If sewage is involved, keep food, stock and people away from the area. Health rules for restaurants and food stores are strict, so tell us early.

After-hours calls: what we need to know

A good description saves time. We ask which fixtures are affected, whether the building has a shared main, who owns the building, and whether a tenant or a landlord is on site. A multi-tenant building may have a separate shutoff for each unit, and the landlord may be the one to authorize repairs.

At night, access is the usual delay. If the building is locked or alarmed, have a key holder meet the crew. Same-day appointments cover calls that can wait until morning, and the dispatch line stays open every night and weekend for the ones that cannot.

Grease traps, interceptors and food service

Any kitchen that cooks produces grease, and a grease trap or interceptor is there to catch it before it reaches the sewer. When the trap fills, grease passes through and sets in the building drain. The result is the slow, smelly backup that shows up on the busiest night of the week.

Trap pumping is usually handled by a hauler, but the plumbing around it is ours: baffles, vents, flow control fittings and the drain lines. We look for the root cause of a recurring kitchen backup, whether it is the trap, the lines or how the kitchen is used. A camera check of the main line is often the next step, covered in sewer inspection.

Commercial rules in Kearny and next door

Wastewater in parts of the Meadowlands district and the South Kearny commercial and industrial areas is handled by the Kearny Municipal Utilities Authority, which runs five pumping stations and about 11 miles of sewer, with 24/7 emergency response staff. Flows go on to the Passaic Valley Sewerage Commission for final treatment. If your building is in that area, a backup may involve the KMUA, so ask which agency serves your address.

Next door, Lyndhurst's code requires a permit from its DPW for commercial or industrial discharge to the sanitary or storm sewer. That is a Lyndhurst rule, not Kearny's. For anything else, permit rules vary, so ask the construction code office. Work that involves a permit is inspected before it is closed.

Commercial discharge rules vary by town. According to the Lyndhurst township code, for example, commercial and industrial discharge to a sanitary or storm sewer needs a permit from the DPW, so ask the local office about your property.

Landlord, tenant and who pays

Responsibility depends on the lease and on where the failure is. Many leases make the landlord responsible for the main lines, risers and building systems, and the tenant responsible for fixtures inside the unit. That is a general pattern, not a rule, so read your lease and ask your landlord.

We document what we find so you have a record for the lease or an insurance claim: photos, the failed part, and where it sat in the system. If a repair is the landlord's, we can coordinate with the property manager. We give a clear price before work starts, whoever approves it.

Preventive checks that keep a business open

Most commercial emergencies show warning signs. These checks cost little compared with a closed day.

  • Know where the main shutoff and each branch shutoff is, and make sure staff on every shift do too.
  • Test flushometers and faucets for leaks or running water, since a running toilet in a commercial restroom wastes a lot.
  • Schedule drain jetting for kitchens and multifamily stacks before they back up.
  • Flush water heaters on a schedule and test the relief valves.
  • Have backflow assemblies tested as your water utility or code office requires.

If the building was built decades ago, ask about the condition of the supply and drain lines. A full repiping plan can be done in phases to keep the business open.

Warehouses, offices and Kearny Point

Not every commercial building is a restaurant. Warehouses have long drain runs and few fixtures, which makes a hidden leak easy to miss. Offices have many restrooms and a small kitchen on each floor. Studios and production spaces, like those at the former shipyard now known as Kearny Point, have unusual loads and tight schedules.

Whatever the building, we ask what it is used for and what runs through the pipes. A repair for a print shop is not the same as a repair for a bakery. Across the river, the same questions apply to Harrison storefronts and offices on tight blocks.
