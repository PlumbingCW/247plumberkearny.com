Emergency Water Service Line Repair in Kearny, NJ

A wet patch in the yard with no rain, a meter that spins with every tap closed, or a house that suddenly has no water can all point to a failed water service line, and our 24/7 dispatch line in Kearny, NJ takes those calls day and night. The water service line is the buried pipe that carries drinking water from the main under the street into your building. Repair means finding the failed spot, then fixing that section or replacing the line with modern pipe.

When a water service line failure is an emergency

A service line fails quietly at first. It becomes urgent when water is surfacing, when pressure drops to a trickle, or when a basement wall is seeping where the line comes in. Those calls go to the front of the schedule at night and on weekends, and same-day appointments are available for a slow leak that can safely wait.

  1. Close the main shutoff valve inside the house, usually on the wall nearest the street. Turn the handle until it stops.
  2. Open one cold tap on the lowest floor to drain the pipes and relieve pressure, then leave it open.
  3. Read the water meter and write the numbers down. If they keep climbing with the valve closed, the leak is ahead of your valve.
  4. If water is coming up in the street or sidewalk, or you cannot reach your valve, call Veolia at (201) 955-7406. Nights and weekends that number reaches a 24/7 answering service.
  5. Call us at (973) 488-5410 and tell us where the line enters the building and where the wet ground is.

Do not dig on your own. A shovel through a wet yard is a common way to hit a gas line or a cable that nobody marked.

Telling a service line leak from a plumbing leak inside

The meter is the dividing line. Close the main valve inside the house and watch the meter dial. If it stops, the leak is in your house plumbing. If it keeps turning, water is escaping between the meter and the valve, or the meter itself is the problem.

Outside signs help too. A soft, green or constantly damp strip of lawn that runs toward the street, a sinkhole forming in the soil, or a faint hiss near the foundation all point to the buried line. If you have none of these but your bill has climbed, leak detection pinpoints the loss without trenching the whole yard.

Spot repair, sleeve or full replacement

Not every failed line needs to be dug up from the street to the basement. The right fix depends on what failed and how much of the pipe is in the same condition.

One pinhole or a split at a single spot on otherwise sound pipeCut out the bad section and splice in new pipe with approved fittingsQuickest and least digging, but a second failure nearby is possible on old pipe

Trenchless methods such as pipe bursting exist, but they need space at both ends and suit long, straight, deep runs. For short residential lines, a trench is often the simpler and cheaper option.

Why buried water lines fail

Galvanized steel rusts from the inside until the opening narrows and the wall thins. Copper can develop pinholes where soil chemistry or stray electrical current attacks it. Old lead pipe seldom bursts, but its joints and connections can weep. Plastic fails differently, usually at a fitting or where a rock has pressed on it for decades.

Freeze damage is its own category. A line laid too shallow, or one that passes through an unheated crawlspace or garage wall, can crack when the ground and air get cold enough. If a frozen line is the problem, thaw it carefully and have it inspected afterward, since a split pipe leaks only once the ice melts.

Who owns which part of the line

In most towns the water utility owns the main in the street and the connection to it, while the property owner owns the pipe from there to the building. Where exactly the ownership line falls varies, so we do not assume it. Ask the Town of Kearny Water Department or Veolia before you pay for work near the curb.

Kearny's water comes from the North Jersey District Water Supply Commission, delivered through the town's distribution system, with Veolia handling billing and customer service. A main break, a broken hydrant or a street leak is theirs to fix. If the leak is on the building side of the connection, it is yours, and we do that repair.

Signs your service line is going

Most failures give warning. These are the signs worth acting on before the line lets go in the middle of the night.

  • Water pressure that has dropped across the whole house, not just at one tap.
  • Brown or rust-colored water after the house has been unused for a few hours.
  • A water bill that jumps with no change in how many people live in the house.
  • A patch of lawn that stays wet or sunken in dry weather.
  • The sound of running water at night when every fixture is closed.

If two or more of these match, call before the next freeze. A line that already has pinholes is more likely to split when it gets cold. Related problems inside the walls are covered under burst pipe repair.

Permits and the dig itself

New Jersey's Uniform Construction Code requires a construction permit for plumbing work on existing buildings, and the only listed exemption is ordinary maintenance, which includes routine repair. Whether a full line replacement counts depends on the scope, so ask the Kearny construction code office. Permit rules vary, and all permits in town require an inspection.

Lyndhurst's code is a useful comparison. It requires service connections to be laid at least 3.5 feet deep and wants proof of lead service pipe replacement for a Certificate of Occupancy. That is Lyndhurst law, not Kearny's, but it shows what inspectors in this area look for. Before any trench goes in, the utilities are marked, and the new line is pressure tested before backfill.
