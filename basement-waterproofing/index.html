Basement Waterproofing in Kearny, NJ

A wet or damp basement has several possible causes, and the right fix depends on where the water comes from. Plumbers handle interior water management, such as drains, sump pits and pumps. Digging outside the foundation is usually another trade.

Where basement water actually comes from

Water enters a basement in a few ways. Groundwater presses on the foundation and finds its way in at cracks, the joint where the wall meets the floor (the cove joint) and gaps around pipes. Surface water arrives from poor grading, short downspouts and window wells that fill. Condensation forms on cold walls and pipes in humid weather.

The fourth cause is plumbing: a leaking supply line, a weeping drain or a failed water heater. People often start with the biggest answer, but it is smarter to start with the cheapest question. Is this water coming from my own pipes?

Rule out plumbing and condensation first

Before paying for a waterproofing system, spend an hour on these checks. They cost almost nothing.

  • Note your meter reading, shut off every fixture for an hour and check it again. Movement means a leak.
  • Tape a square of aluminum foil tightly to a damp wall for a day. Moisture on the room side means condensation. Moisture under the foil means water through the wall.
  • Look for stains below pipes, water heaters, laundry boxes and washing machine hoses.
  • Check whether the dampness follows a rain event or appears with no weather at all.
  • Smell for sewage, which points to a drain or lateral issue, not groundwater.

If the meter moves with fixtures off, call for leak detection before anything else.

How an interior perimeter drain works

A perimeter drain is a channel cut along the inside edge of the basement floor, along the wall. A perforated pipe, often called drain tile, sits in gravel in that channel and slopes to a sump pit. New concrete covers it, and a wall flashing or baffle directs any water running down the wall into the channel.

It does not stop water from entering the foundation. It gives water somewhere to go before it reaches the floor, and then a pump moves it out. That is why a drain is only as good as the pump and the discharge route that goes with it.

Sump pits and pumps

A sump pit is a basin, usually 18 to 24 inches across, set below the floor level. A submersible pump sits at the bottom with a float switch that turns it on as water rises. A check valve in the discharge line keeps pumped water from running back in.

For anything important, we suggest a pump on a GFCI protected outlet, a high water alarm and a battery backup, since sump pumps tend to work hardest when storms and outages overlap. Our sump pump installation and repair page covers sizing, replacement and the signs of a tired pump.

Sealers, paint and what they cannot do

Masonry sealers and waterproof paints are sold in every hardware store. They can slow dampness, stop chalky white deposits and reduce a musty smell on dry-ish walls. They cannot hold back water under pressure, and they often peel when it pushes through from behind.

We say this because it is the most common waste of money we see described. If the wall is truly wet, use a drain or an exterior fix. If it is just damp, a sealer plus better gutters might be all it needs.

What a plumber does and does not do

We handle interior water management: perimeter drains, sump pits and pumps, discharge piping, backwater valves and the plumbing connections around them. We can help you decide whether those fit your basement.

Exterior excavation, foundation membranes, major crack repair in structural walls and regrading are usually done by another trade. If your problem needs that, we will say so and help you describe it to a contractor. We do not promise a basement that is perfectly dry in every condition, and neither should anyone else.

According to the Lyndhurst township code, stormwater and unpolluted drainage may not go into the sanitary sewer. Kearny's rule is not in our verified sources, so ask the construction code office before you route a sump discharge.

Gutters, grading and other cheap fixes

Many wet basements improve with simple outside work. Downspouts should discharge several feet away from the foundation, not at the wall. The ground should slope away from the house. Gutters should be clear, and window wells should drain or have covers.

These changes cost little compared with a drain system. They also help the system you install later work less. Nothing on this list is a plumbing job, and any of them is worth trying before an interior drain.

Low ground and drainage near the rivers

Kearny sits beside the Passaic and Hackensack rivers with Meadowlands wetlands to the east. As a general matter, low-lying property near rivers and wetlands drains slowly after heavy rain, and a sump pump may run more often there. We cannot tell you the groundwater depth at your address, and we do not make claims about it. A contractor can check the soil at your site.

Know where your sump discharges. Lyndhurst's township code, for example, bars stormwater and unpolluted drainage from the sanitary sewer, and other towns have similar rules. Ask the construction code office before connecting a sump to any drain. For a nearby view, see our Lyndhurst page.

Questions to ask any contractor

Whoever you hire, a few questions reveal how honest the plan is.

  • What is the source of the water, and how did you decide?
  • Where will the pumped water discharge, and is that allowed in this town?
  • What happens if the power goes out during a storm?
  • Is the permit included, and who schedules the inspection?
  • What part of this is not covered by your trade, and who handles it?

If a sewer line is involved, a sewer camera inspection separates groundwater from a leaking lateral.
